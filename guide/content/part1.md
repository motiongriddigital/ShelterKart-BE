## Why I started ShelterKart

For most of my career I worked as a real estate strategist. I advised buyers, sellers, investors, and the people who sat between them. I worked across land, commercial and residential property, in several countries, on deals large and small. Those years taught me a set of principles, and I still hold them.

Look past the brochure. A brochure is written to sell, and it hides more than it reveals. True value takes real scrutiny of the fundamentals.

Deals are made by people. Every transaction has human motivations behind it, and understanding them is often what unlocks a deal that has stalled.

Patience is a financial skill. Impulse buys produce regret. Informed, patient decisions tend to hold their value.

I don't oversell, overpromise or manufacture urgency. If something doesn't make sense, I say so, even when it costs me the deal. I value clarity, structure and responsibility far more than volume.

What I noticed, across every segment I worked in, is that the people with the most information about a property are the people selling it. The people with the most at stake, the buyers, have the least. A land buyer, a commercial tenant, a residential purchaser and an investor all face the same gap: the record that determines whether a deal is sound is scattered across registries, filings, court listings and approvals, and no one is paid to read it for them.

Every party in a transaction is paid when it closes. That is not a criticism of anyone. It is simply the structure of the market, and it means that the one party with no stake in the outcome is usually the buyer's own research, which is often the weakest part of the process.

ShelterKart exists to close that gap. We read the public record on a property and on the company behind it. We show what the record says, what it doesn't say, and where the sources disagree. Then we turn every gap into a question to put to the people who should answer it, with a clear description of what a good answer looks like.

We will never tell you whether to buy. That decision belongs to you, and you should make it with your own advisers.

## Chapter 1: Before you shortlist: what you are really buying

**The friend.** You are not buying a flat, a plot or an office. You are buying a number of years of your life in one place, a promise about the future, and a liability you will carry for a long time. Most expensive mistakes are made before the first site visit: in the budget, the timeline and the reason.

**The philosopher.** Start with one question: what is this purchase for? A home to live in, a property to rent out, land to hold, an asset to sell later. Each has a different test.

- **To live in:** judged on the years you will actually use it.
- **To rent out:** judged on yield, vacancy and how easily it can be let.
- **To hold:** judged on how easily you can exit, and what it costs to wait.
- **To resell:** judged on liquidity, and on what the next buyer will want.

Pick one primary purpose and write it down. Most regret comes from buying for one reason and discovering you needed another. Then ask a second question: what would make me regret this in five years?

**The mathematician.** The price is where the cost begins, not where it ends.

> Total cost of owning = price + one-time costs + running costs + cost of money − what you can sell it for.

- **One-time costs:** transfer or stamp taxes, registration, legal fees, broker fees, loan processing, furnishing and moving. Depending on the market these add a few percent of the price, or more than ten percent. Ask a local adviser for your market's figures. Do not accept a round number from a seller.
- **Running costs:** maintenance or service charges, property tax, insurance, utilities and repairs.
- **Cost of money:** loan interest, plus what the cash you have tied up could have earned elsewhere.

Suppose the price is 100 and the one-time costs are 8. You have spent 108 before you have lived there a day.

Run two stress tests before you go further:

1. **Rates and income.** Raise the loan rate by two percentage points and cut your household income by a fifth. Can you carry the payments for a full year? If not, the purchase is too tight.
2. **Waiting.** If the home is delayed by twelve months, what do you pay in rent and interest while you wait? Chapter 6 works this out.

**The lawyer.** At this stage nothing should bind you, but some things do. A booking form or a token payment can be a contract. Before you pay anything, ask whether it is refundable, what happens to it if you change your mind, and exactly what you are signing. In many markets the booking form is the seller's own draft of the deal. If it matters, have a lawyer read it first.

**The consultant.** Write three lists before you see a brochure:

- **Must-haves:** without these you will not buy.
- **Nice-to-haves:** things you would like.
- **Deal-breakers:** things that end the conversation.

Sellers sell nice-to-haves, because they are easy to photograph. Your must-haves and deal-breakers are what protect you. When you tour, check what you see against your lists, not against the show home.

**The emotional beat.** The show home is designed to make you feel something. The lighting, the furniture and the view from the model window are not the home you will receive. It is fine to feel the pull. Name it: "I like how this feels. Is it in my must-haves?" Then take the decision home and sleep on it.

## Chapter 2: Who is actually selling to you

**The friend.** The name on the brochure is often not the company that signs your contract. Many large developers build each project through a separate company created for that project and owned by a bigger group. The brand is marketing. The contract is with the legal entity. Your rights run against the entity that signs, so that is the one to know.

**The lawyer.** Identify four parties and keep them apart:

- **The landowner:** who holds the title to the land.
- **The developer:** who has the right to build and sell on it, by ownership, a joint venture or a development agreement.
- **The project company:** the legal entity that signs your agreement and receives your money.
- **The builder:** who constructs it.

These can be one company or four. For each, get the legal name, the registration number and the registered address. Then ask which of them gives you each promise: title, completion, quality and the repair of defects. In many markets the agreement and the payment receipts show which entity is the seller. Check that the name on every receipt matches the name on the agreement.

**The mathematician.** Two simple measures show how much stands behind a promise.

1. **Distance.** Count the companies between the brand and the entity that signs. Every layer is a place where the brand's reputation and the legal obligation can separate.
2. **Whose money it is.** Divide the buyer money collected by the capital the company itself put in. If buyers have funded most of the project, buyers are in effect its lenders. That is common, and it is not wrong. But it means the promise is only as strong as the group's willingness to support it, so ask who stands behind the project company.

Both are questions, not verdicts.

**The engineer.** Ask who builds, and what they have built. A builder's finished projects, with their promised and actual completion dates and their defect history, tell you more than a brochure about the next one. Visit a completed project and talk to the people who live there.

**The consultant.** Ask for these in writing:

- The legal name and registration number of the selling company, the landowner and the builder.
- The land title, or the development agreement that gives the right to sell.
- A list of completed projects, with the promised and the actual completion dates.
- A schedule of legal cases involving the company and the project, with their status.
- The account your payments go into, and whether the law in this market requires it to be a protected or designated account.

A good answer arrives within a day, with documents. A weak answer is "our brand is trusted by thousands."

**The philosopher.** A reputation is a promise made by a name. A contract is made by an entity. Trust the name if you like. Sign only with the entity, and make sure the entity can keep the promise.

**The emotional beat.** The salesperson may be warm, capable and honest. That person is still not the party you would take to court. Keep the warmth. Keep the paper.

### What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

- The registered seller, compared with the name the project is marketed under.
- The people and addresses the seller's company shares with other companies in its group, shown as facts and never as conclusions.
- Completion dates promised and filed on the record.
- Cases and filings that official records state, each with its link and date.

Where the record is silent, the report says so and gives you the question to ask.

### Questions to put to the seller's team

1. Which exact legal entity signs my agreement and receives my money?
2. Who owns the land, and what gives you the right to sell it?
3. Which company builds, and which projects has it completed?
4. For your last three completed projects, what were the promised and the actual completion dates?
5. Which legal cases involve the company or this project, and what is their status?
6. Into which account do my payments go, and who can withdraw from it?
7. If the project company cannot finish, who stands behind the promise?

## Chapter 3: The approvals: what must exist before a unit is sold

**The friend.** A beautiful tower can be sold before every permission to build it exists. Approvals are the skeleton of the deal. If one is missing, the building can be stopped, fined or left half-finished, and you are the one holding the payment receipts.

**The lawyer.** In most markets the stack looks like this, though the names and the order differ:

- **The right to the land:** title, or a development right over it.
- **Planning or zoning permission:** what may be built, how high and how dense.
- **The building permit or commencement approval,** often phase by phase.
- **Safety and utility clearances:** fire, environmental, aviation, water and power, depending on the project.
- **Registration with the property regulator,** where one exists, with its project number, promised dates and approved area.
- **At the end, the completion or occupancy certificate,** without which you may not be allowed to move in or to receive a clean title.

Two rules. Permission for one phase is not permission for the next. And every approval has a date and an expiry, so check both.

**The mathematician.** Check the arithmetic of area. Suppose you are told the number of homes and the area of each, and the approvals state the total area that may be built.

> Homes sold × area per home ≤ approved area × usable share

The usable share is below 100% because corridors, lifts, stairs, walls and shared spaces take part of every building. What counts as "area" varies by market, so ask which definition each number uses.

For example, 100 homes of 70 square metres each is 7,000 of sold area. If the approved total is 6,000, the sold area is larger than the approved area, and the numbers do not reconcile.

That is not proof of wrongdoing. Areas are measured in different ways, and homes can be counted across phases. But it is a question you should have answered before you pay.

**The engineer.** Read the approved drawings, not the brochure. Check the number of floors, the height, the parking, the fire exits and the open space against what you were shown. Ask who the structural engineer of record is, and where the structural design approval sits. The floors in the brochure and the floors in the approval should match.

**The consultant.** Ask to see each approval in the original, with its date and validity. Then look it up yourself on the regulator's public page, so you are comparing the paper you were handed with the official record. Ask what is still pending, and by when. A good answer is a list of documents with dates. A weak answer is "all approvals are in place," with no papers.

**The philosopher.** A promise without permission is a hope. Ask for the permission before you invest the hope.

**The emotional beat.** Asking for papers when everyone is smiling can feel rude. It is not. A good seller expects it and answers readily. A seller who bristles is telling you something.

### What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

- The project's registration with the regulator, with its dates and any extensions.
- Whether the area sold reconciles with the approved area, where both are on the record.
- The number of floors marketed, against the number approved.
- Whether the approvals on the record cover the phase you are buying.

Where documents are not public, the report says so and tells you what to ask for.

### Questions to put to the seller's team

1. Which approvals are in place for the phase I am buying, with their dates and expiry?
2. What is the project's registration number with the regulator, and where can I verify it?
3. What is the total approved area, and how does it compare with the area of the homes on sale?
4. How many floors are approved, and does that match the brochure?
5. Which approvals are still pending, and when are they expected?
6. Who is the structural engineer of record, and where is the design approval?
7. When will the completion or occupancy certificate be applied for, and by whom?
