## Chapter 10: Buying from abroad

**The friend.** Distance multiplies every risk in this book. You cannot see the site, you may not read the language of the contract, and you cannot walk into the registry. What you do have is time, and the freedom to refuse haste. Use both.

**The lawyer.** Five steps carry most of the protection.

1. **Hire your own local lawyer.** Do not use one recommended by the seller, or by an agent who is paid when the sale completes. Confirm that the lawyer is licensed in that country. Ask them to check the seller, the title and the approvals, to read the agreement, and to handle registration and tax advice.
2. **Be careful with a power of attorney.** It gives another person legal authority over your purchase. A broad one can allow them to sell or mortgage your property. Limit it by scope, purpose and duration, name someone you trust, and ask your lawyer how it can be revoked.
3. **Check the foreign-ownership rules before you fall in love.** Some countries restrict foreign buyers by area, property type or quota, allow leasehold only, or require approvals or extra taxes.
4. **Check the rules on money.** Find out what is required to bring funds in, what proof of source is needed, and what is required to take the proceeds of a sale out of the country.
5. **Check the paperwork and succession.** Documents signed abroad often need notarising, an apostille or consular legalisation, and a certified translation. Local inheritance law may decide who receives the property, and it may differ from the law at home.

**The mathematician.** Count the whole cost across borders:

> All-in cost = price converted at the rate you actually receive + transfer fees + taxes and legal fees + the cost of visits

If you plan to rent the property out, measure its real return:

> Net yield = (yearly rent − management fee − vacancy loss − running costs − tax) ÷ all-in price

Suppose the all-in price is 100, the yearly rent is 6, the management fee is 0.6, one month of vacancy costs 0.5, running costs are 1 and tax is 0.8. The net yield is (6 − 0.6 − 0.5 − 1 − 0.8) ÷ 100, which is 3.1%. The headline yield of 6% was nearly twice the real one.

**The engineer.** You need eyes on the site that are not paid by the seller. Hire an independent inspector for site visits and dated photographs. Ask for a live video walk-through. Compare what you see with the certified schedule from Chapter 6.

**The consultant.** Seven rules protect your money:

1. Pay only to the account named in the agreement, in the name of the selling entity.
2. Verify the account details by calling a number you found yourself, not one in an email or message.
3. Treat any change of bank details as a warning, and confirm it by phone.
4. Check any agent's licence on the regulator's public register.
5. Never pay because "the price rises tomorrow."
6. Keep every instruction and promise in writing.
7. Do not rely on a "friend of the developer" who offers a better deal.

**The philosopher.** Distance is a cost. Count it. Do not simply endure it.

**The emotional beat.** For many buyers abroad, a purchase is also about home: memory, belonging, family. That is a good reason to buy, and a risky one. When the heart is already there, the head has to stay at the table. Ask someone you trust, who does not share your longing, to read the file.

## Chapter 11: When something goes wrong: delay, defects and disputes

**The friend.** Most purchases end well. Plan for the ones that do not, because the buyers who come through best are the ones who prepared before they needed to.

**The lawyer.** Disputes move up a ladder. Start on the lowest rung that works.

1. **A written notice to the seller:** the facts, what you want, and a date for the reply.
2. **The seller's complaint channel,** if the agreement names one.
3. **The regulator's complaint process,** where the market has a property regulator.
4. **Mediation or arbitration,** if the agreement or the law provides for it.
5. **Court.**

Three cautions apply in every market. Claims have time limits, which run from particular events, so act early. Do not stop paying without legal advice, because the agreement may let the seller cancel or charge you for a late payment. And keep records of everything:

- The signed agreement with every schedule.
- Every receipt and bank statement.
- Emails and messages.
- Dated photographs and your site-visit notes.
- A note of each call: date, who, and what was said.

Use writing wherever you can. Confirm any call by email, and send formal notices by a method that proves delivery. Other buyers in the same project may share your problem. Where the law allows, acting together can share the cost.

**The mathematician.** Before you start a dispute, estimate what it is worth:

> Expected net outcome = (chance of success × amount recovered) − (fees and other costs) − the value of your time

Suppose the claim is 50, the chance of success is 60%, costs are 8 and your time is worth 2. The expected net outcome is 0.6 × 50 − 8 − 2 = 20. If the chance falls to 30%, it is 0.3 × 50 − 10 = 5.

Use the same sum to judge an offer. A settlement of 22 today beats an expected 20 in three years, because money later is worth less than money now (Chapter 5). Ask your lawyer for an honest range for the chance of success, and treat a confident single number with caution.

**The engineer.** For defects, document first and argue second. Take dated photographs, ask an independent inspector for a written report, and notify the seller in writing within the defect liability period. Ask for a repair schedule with dates. At handover, do not accept possession "in full satisfaction" while snags remain. List them in the handover record.

**The consultant.** Short, specific messages work best. Three templates:

- "The agreement states possession by [date]. Please confirm in writing the revised date, the reason, and the compensation due under clause [number]."
- "Please confirm receipt of this notice by [date]."
- "I noted the following defects on [date]. Please send a repair schedule by [date]."

A good answer is dated, specific and in writing. A weak answer is a promise by phone.

**The philosopher.** Conflict is expensive and clarity is cheap. Most disputes cost less the earlier they are named.

**The emotional beat.** People wait too long. They hope, they feel embarrassed to complain, or they fear making things worse. The first written question is the cheapest step you will ever take. Talk to the other buyers. Almost no one is alone in this.

## Chapter 12: Possession and the first year

**The friend.** The keys feel like the end. They are the point at which your leverage is smallest and your paperwork is least complete. Handover is a process with a checklist, and the first year is when hidden defects appear.

**The lawyer.** Before you accept possession, ask for these and keep every document:

- **The completion or occupancy certificate,** under whatever name your market uses. Ask your lawyer what must exist before you may move in.
- **The final measurement,** compared with the area in the agreement.
- **A signed snag list,** with a date for each repair.
- **The start date of the defect liability period,** in writing.
- **Warranty documents, manuals and the as-built drawings,** which show where the pipes and cables run.
- **Meter readings** for electricity, water and gas, and the transfer of utilities to your name.
- **Final payments and deposits,** exactly as the agreement states them, each with a receipt.
- **Title.** The keys are not ownership. Check that the transfer is registered in your name, collect the originals, and have your lawyer confirm there is no charge or claim against the property.
- **The owners' association:** how and when it is formed, when the builder hands over control, who holds the reserve fund, and where the accounts are.

Then arrange insurance, register for property tax and take photographs of every room before the furniture arrives.

**The mathematician.** Reconcile two things.

**Area.** Compare the area delivered with the area sold:

> Adjustment = (area sold − area delivered) × price per unit of area

Suppose 90 was sold, 87 is delivered and the price is 10 per unit. The shortfall is 3 and the adjustment is 30. Check the tolerance the agreement allows before you claim.

**Money.** Add up every receipt and check that the total equals what the agreement says you should have paid by now.

Then run your first-year budget:

> Year-one cost = loan payments + service charges + property tax + insurance + utilities + repairs

Compare it with the plan you made in Chapter 1, and note every line that differs.

**The engineer.** Snag the home room by room, with an independent inspector if you can:

- **Walls and ceilings:** cracks, damp patches and stains.
- **Doors and windows:** they open, close and lock, with even gaps.
- **Electrical:** every socket and switch, and the circuit breakers.
- **Plumbing:** run every tap, flush every toilet, check pressure and drainage, and look under sinks for leaks.
- **Bathrooms and balconies:** ask the inspector to run a water test, and look for slope towards the drains.
- **Floors:** tap tiles for hollow sounds, and check that they are level.
- **Ventilation and finishes:** extractor fans, paint and joints.

Photograph every defect, with the date.

**The consultant.** Run the first year on a schedule:

- **Week 1:** the snag list, signed.
- **Month 1:** check that every repair was done.
- **Month 3:** check for damp after the first heavy rain or cold spell.
- **Two months before the defect period ends:** a full inspection, and a written list sent to the seller.
- **Throughout:** join the owners' association and read its accounts.

**The philosopher.** The purchase ends when the paperwork is clean, not when the keys arrive.

**The emotional beat.** The moment of the keys is joyful, and that is when people sign away their rights. Do not sign a blanket "accepted in full satisfaction" form while snags remain. Enjoy the day, and keep the list.
