## Chapter 7: The build: area, specification and the site visit

**The friend.** You are buying a building that will stand for decades, not a brochure. Quality is invisible until the first heavy rain, the first cold winter or the first year of use. That is why it has to be checked on paper and on site before you pay.

**The mathematician.** Three area terms cause most confusion. Markets use different names for them, so ask for the definition each time:

- **Carpet or net internal area:** the floor inside your walls, where you can stand and place furniture.
- **Built-up area:** the carpet area plus the thickness of the walls, balconies and similar spaces.
- **Saleable or super built-up area:** the built-up area plus your share of the common spaces, such as lobbies, stairs, lifts and the plant rooms.

The gap between the first and the last is the loading factor:

> Loading factor = (saleable area − carpet area) ÷ carpet area

Suppose the carpet area is 60 and the saleable area is 90. The loading factor is 50%. At a price of 10 per saleable unit, you pay 900 in all, and 300 of it is for space outside your walls. Compare homes by price per unit of carpet area, never by the larger number in the brochure.

The International Property Measurement Standards exist so that floor areas can be compared across markets. Many markets still use their own definitions, so measure what you are given.

**The engineer.** Ask for evidence on each of these, and have an independent professional read it with you:

- **Foundations and structure:** the soil investigation, the foundation type, the structural system, the design code and the engineer of record. Where earthquakes, strong winds or floods are a risk, ask how the design addresses them.
- **Fire safety:** the number and width of stairs, the exits, and the fire-fighting systems.
- **Waterproofing:** bathrooms, terraces, basements and the facade. Water damage is among the most common defects.
- **Light and air:** the orientation of the windows and the direction of the afternoon sun, cross-ventilation, and the clear ceiling height inside a room, which is lower than the floor-to-floor height.
- **Services:** the source and storage of water, sewage treatment, electrical capacity and backup, and whether the lifts are enough for the number of residents.
- **Parking and access:** the ratio of spaces to homes, and step-free access.
- **Energy:** where an energy rating exists for new buildings, ask for it.

**The site visit.**

1. Stand in a finished, empty unit of the same type, not in the furnished show home.
2. Go at two different times of day, and in the rain if you can.
3. Look at the neighbours: the plots around the site, and what may be built on them.
4. Visit a completed project by the same builder that is three to five years old. Look for cracks, stains, seepage and how the common areas are kept.
5. Hire an independent inspector or surveyor, who is not paid by the seller, before your final payment and again at handover.

**The lawyer.** The specification schedule in the agreement is a contract. The brochure is not. Check for "or equivalent" and for rights to substitute materials. Ask what warranties cover the structure, the waterproofing and the fittings, who gives them, and for how long.

**The consultant.** Ask for the specification by brand, grade and thickness. Ask for a sample of each major finish. Ask to see the structural design approval. A good answer is a document, a sample or a visit. A weak answer is "it is premium quality."

**The philosopher.** A home is the building you will live in, not the building you were shown.

**The emotional beat.** A show home is furnished, lit and staged to make you feel something. Enjoy it, and then ask to stand in an empty, finished unit.

### What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

- The number of floors marketed, against the number approved.
- The areas stated, against the areas in the registered documents.
- Which approvals and specifications appear on the record.

Where an item is not on the record, the report turns it into a question for the seller's team.

### Questions to put to the seller's team

1. How are the carpet, built-up and saleable areas of my home measured, and what is the carpet area?
2. What is the loading factor?
3. What is the clear ceiling height inside the rooms?
4. Who is the structural engineer of record, and what is the design code?
5. What waterproofing method is used on bathrooms, terraces and basements, and what warranty applies?
6. Where is the specification listed, by brand, grade and thickness?
7. Can I stand in a finished, empty unit and visit a completed project of yours?
8. May an independent inspector of my choice inspect my home before the final payment?

## Chapter 8: The life you will actually live: amenities, neighbourhood and your family's timeline

**The friend.** Amenities sell homes. The brochure shows a swimming pool full of laughing children, a lawn, a clubhouse. But you will not move into the photograph. You will move into the life you have on the day the keys arrive, and that life may not contain the people in the picture.

**The mathematician.** Start with the ages of everyone in your household on the day you will actually move in.

> Age at possession = age today + years until possession

Use the expected date from Chapter 6, not the promised one. A child of 10 and a promise of five years gives 15. If the realistic wait is six years, it gives 16.

Now take an amenity with an age band, such as a play area:

> Usable years = the upper age of the equipment − age at possession, never less than zero

If the equipment is rated up to age 12, the usable years for that child are 12 − 15, which is zero. The play area is still a good feature. It is simply no longer a feature for that child.

Put a rough value on any amenity this way:

> Value to you ≈ usable years × how often you will use it × what the same use costs elsewhere × the chance it arrives on time

The last term matters. An amenity in a late phase may arrive after the years in which you would have used it.

The same sums apply to the people at the other end of life. A lift, step-free access and a shaded walking path matter more with every year, so run the ages of older family members forward as well.

Then price the running cost. Residents pay for amenities through service charges, whether or not they use them. If a club costs 600,000 a year to run and 300 homes share it, each home pays 2,000 a year.

And price your commute:

> Hours a year = round-trip hours a day × working days a year

Two hours a day over 250 days is 500 hours a year, about twenty full days. Set that against any saving in price.

**The engineer.** Ask which phase delivers each amenity, and whether it appears in the approved plans. An amenity on a podium, a roof or the last block depends on the construction of that structure. Ask who will maintain it and how the cost will be shared.

**The lawyer.** An amenity counts if it is named in the agreement's schedule. Check whether the use of an amenity is included in the price or sold as a separate membership, who owns the common areas, and what happens if an amenity is not delivered or is changed.

**The consultant.** Value what exists today. A planned metro line, school, hospital or road has its own timeline, and the same planning fallacy applies to it. Check the official plans for the land around the project: what could be built next door, what could block a view or add noise or traffic.

Then do this exercise. For each person in your household, write their age at possession, five years later and ten years later. Beside each, list the five amenities they would actually use. Compare your lists with the five the brochure shows.

Also think about resale. A home that suits only one stage of life narrows the pool of people who will want to buy it from you.

**The philosopher.** Value is a relationship between a thing and a life. The same pool is worth different things to a five-year-old and a fifteen-year-old.

**The emotional beat.** Do not buy for the child you imagine. Buy for the family you will be. And resist the guilt that says a good parent would never turn down the pool.

### What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

- Which amenities appear in the project's registered documents, and which phase each belongs to.
- Infrastructure claims, such as nearby transport or schools, against the official plans, where those are on the record.

Where the record cannot show an amenity or its phase, the report turns it into a question for the seller's team.

### Questions to put to the seller's team

1. Which amenities are listed in the agreement, and in which phase is each delivered?
2. What age range is the play equipment designed for?
3. What will each amenity cost residents each year, and who will run it?
4. If an amenity is late or changed, what do I receive?
5. Which amenities are included in the price, and which are paid memberships?
6. What has been approved for the land and roads around the project?
7. Which planned infrastructure has an approved date, and which is only proposed?
8. When do residents take over the management of the common areas?

## Chapter 9: Financing, tax and currency

**The friend.** Most buyers borrow. The loan is often the largest commitment you will make, and it outlasts the excitement of the purchase by twenty years. Treat it as a decision of its own, not a detail of the purchase.

**The mathematician.** A loan with equal monthly payments costs:

> Payment = L × r × (1 + r)^n ÷ ((1 + r)^n − 1)

Here L is the amount borrowed, r is the monthly interest rate (the yearly rate ÷ 12) and n is the number of months.

Borrow 100 for 20 years at 8% a year. The payment is 0.836 a month for each 100 borrowed. Over 240 months you repay about 200.7, so the interest is about 100.7. You repay roughly twice what you borrowed.

Now raise the rate to 10%. The payment becomes 0.965 a month, and the total repaid rises to about 231.6. A two-point rise in the rate lifts the monthly payment by about 15%. This is the stress test from Chapter 1.

For a home that is still being built, lenders often pay the seller in stages as work is certified. You pay interest on what has been drawn so far, and you may also still pay rent. That is the monthly cost of waiting from Chapter 4, and it is why a late home costs more than it first appears.

If the price is in a currency other than the one you earn, the exchange rate is part of the price.

> Cost in your currency = price ÷ the rate at the time you pay

If your currency falls 10% against the currency of the price, each payment of 100 costs you 100 ÷ 0.9 = 111, about 11% more. The spread matters too. Compare the rate a bank or transfer provider gives you with the mid-market rate:

> Cost of conversion = (mid-market rate − the rate you receive) ÷ mid-market rate

Payments spread over years average the rate, but they also carry its risk. Ask a bank about fixing the rate in advance for known payments.

**The lawyer.** Read the loan agreement as carefully as the sale agreement:

- **Fixed or floating rate,** and how often a floating rate resets.
- **Prepayment charges** if you repay early or refinance.
- **What happens if the project is delayed:** do you keep paying interest, and on what amount?
- **The lender's claim on the property,** and what happens if the seller fails.
- **Insurance** the lender requires, and who pays for it.
- **Source-of-funds checks.** Expect to document where your deposit came from. Anti-money-laundering rules apply in most markets.

In some markets lenders keep lists of projects they have approved. An approval is a useful signal and not a guarantee of quality or delivery.

**The consultant.** Tax belongs in the first sum, not the last. Ask a local tax adviser to price the whole cycle:

- **Buying:** transfer, stamp or registration taxes, and in some markets sales tax or VAT on new buildings.
- **Holding:** annual property taxes, and tax on any rent you receive.
- **Selling:** tax on the gain, and whether the length of time you hold the property or your residency changes it.

Do not count on a tax deduction for your loan until an adviser has confirmed that it applies to you.

**The philosopher.** Debt is a promise your present self makes on behalf of your future self. Make sure your future self was consulted.

**The emotional beat.** A bank's approval feels like a verdict, but it is not one. Lenders approve what you can repay under their rules, not what you can comfortably live with. The number you are comfortable with is usually lower.

### What ShelterKart checks for a project like this

Financing is outside what a public-record report can check, and the report does not tell you whether to borrow. It helps in one way: it shows the seller's legal identity and the dates on the record, which your lender will also test.

### Questions to put to the lender and the seller

1. Is the rate fixed or floating, and when does it reset?
2. What are the prepayment charges?
3. If the home is delayed, what do I pay each month, and on what amount?
4. Has the lender approved this project, and does that approval cover my unit?
5. What are the total taxes and charges for buying, holding and selling?
6. If my currency falls 10%, what does the next payment cost me?
7. Can the rate for known future payments be fixed in advance?
8. Which documents will I need to show where my money came from?
