## Chapter 4: The agreement: the only document that counts

**The friend.** Everything said in the sales office is a conversation. The agreement is the contract. If a promise is not in it, treat it as not made. Read it slowly, at home, before you pay anything beyond a refundable booking amount.

**The lawyer.** These clauses most often decide how a purchase goes. The wording and the legal minimums differ by market, so local counsel decides what applies to you.

- **Parties and the property:** exactly who, and a description precise enough that nothing can be swapped.
- **Price, and what it includes:** and what it leaves out, such as parking, club membership, taxes and connection charges.
- **The payment schedule:** tied to dates, or tied to stages of construction? A stage-linked schedule keeps your payments behind the work.
- **The possession date,** any grace period, and what counts as "possession."
- **The seller's delay:** the compensation or interest you receive, and your right to cancel and be refunded.
- **Changes:** how far the plans, the area or the specification can change without your consent, and what happens to the price when they do.
- **Your default:** what the seller can keep or charge if you pay late, and whether the same rate applies when the seller is late.
- **Title and transfer:** when ownership passes to you, and what happens to your claim if the seller fails.
- **Defects:** the defect liability period, and who repairs what.
- **Amenities and specifications:** listed in a schedule, not in a brochure.
- **Maintenance:** who runs the building after handover, what you pay, and when the residents take control.
- **Disputes:** the governing law, where disputes are heard, and whether arbitration is required.

In many markets the law sets minimum protections or standard forms for sale agreements. Ask a local lawyer which apply.

**The mathematician.** Work out what a delay costs you each month.

> Money you have paid × your cost of money ÷ 12 + rent you are still paying

Compare that with what the contract gives you for each month of delay. If the contract pays less than your cost, the gap is yours.

Suppose you have paid 50 units, your cost of money is 9% a year, and you still pay rent of 0.4 units a month. Your monthly cost is 50 × 0.09 ÷ 12 = 0.375, plus 0.4, which is 0.775. If the contract pays 0.2 a month, you lose 0.575 a month, or 6.9 over a year of delay.

Also check the two directions. If you pay 12% a year when you are late and the seller pays you 6% when it is late, the contract is lopsided.

**The engineer.** The specification schedule should name materials and grades, not adjectives. "Premium tiles" is an adjective. A brand, a thickness and a grade is a specification. Watch for "or equivalent," and ask who decides what is equivalent. Ask what you can see before you commit: a sample, a finished home, a test report. Ask for the structure type, the floor-to-floor height and the waterproofing method.

**The consultant.** These phrases are not accusations. They show where the seller keeps discretion, so question each one:

- "Subject to approvals."
- "At the sole discretion of the developer."
- "As may be decided from time to time."
- "Approximately," next to an area.
- A variation allowance with no limit.
- A penalty that applies only to you.
- A schedule left blank, or "to be provided later."

Practical rules: read every page. Never sign blank pages, or pages with blanks. Get any promise made in the sales office written into the agreement. Ask for changes in writing. Keep a signed copy of everything. Have your own lawyer read it before a token payment becomes a contract.

A good answer to a request for a change is "Yes, we can add that clause," or a clear reason for no. A weak answer is "That is our standard. Nobody changes it."

**The philosopher.** A contract is a plan for the day goodwill runs out. Read it as if the relationship has already gone wrong, because that is the only day it will be used.

**The emotional beat.** Signing day brings pressure: the queue of other buyers, the deadline, the offer that ends today. If an agreement is good for you, it will still be good tomorrow. Take the pages home.

## Chapter 5: The money: price, payment plan and everything on top

**The friend.** Two buyers can pay for the same home and end up with different bills. The number in the brochure is the start of the conversation. The number that matters is what leaves your account, when it leaves, and what you receive for it.

**The mathematician.** A payment plan has a price in time as well as in money. Money paid later is worth less to you than money paid now, because you could use it in the meantime. To compare plans fairly, bring every payment back to today's value:

> Value today of a plan = payment at month 0 + payment at month t ÷ (1 + r)^t + … for every payment

Here r is your own yearly cost of money and t is the time in years.

Suppose Plan A is 100 paid today. Plan B is 20 today, 20 in twelve months and 60 in three years. At r = 8%:

20 + 20 ÷ 1.08 + 60 ÷ 1.08³ = 20 + 18.52 + 47.63 = 86.15

Plan B is worth about 14 less to you than Plan A. Now check the trap. If Plan B carries a price 15% higher, its value today is 86.15 × 1.15 = 99.07, which is the same as Plan A. A deferred plan is only a saving if the price is not raised to pay for it, and only if the home arrives on time.

Compare areas the same way. Offer A is 900 for 60 of carpet area, which is 15 per unit. Offer B is 1,000 for 80 of "saleable" area. Ask B for its carpet area. If it is 55, the price is 18.2 per unit. The bigger number on the brochure was the smaller home. Chapter 7 explains the area terms.

**The lawyer.** Pay only what the agreement states, to the account it names, by a traceable method, and keep a receipt for every payment in the name of the entity that signed. Check these points:

- **The account.** In many markets the law requires buyer money to go into a designated or protected account. Ask whether that applies here and which account it is.
- **The booking amount.** Is it refundable, and is it credited against the price?
- **Taxes and charges.** Which taxes apply to the purchase, who collects them and who pays them?
- **Payments outside the contract.** Money that does not appear in the agreement is not protected by it. If the project fails, you hold no receipt and no claim. In many markets an undeclared payment is also unlawful for the buyer as well as the seller. A discount for paying part outside the books saves less than it risks.

**The engineer.** Match the plan to the work. A stage-linked plan asks for a payment when a defined stage is complete, such as the foundation, a slab or the roof. Ask who certifies each stage, and ask to see the certificate before you pay. Chapter 6 shows how to compare what you have paid with what has been built.

**The consultant.** Ask for the all-in cost on one page, with every line:

- Base price.
- Parking, storage and any premium for floor, view or corner.
- Club, amenity and utility connection charges.
- Maintenance deposits and sinking or reserve fund contributions.
- Transfer, stamp and registration taxes, and legal fees.
- Any brokerage you are being asked to pay.

If a line is "to be advised," ask for the range in writing.

**The philosopher.** Price is what you pay. Value is what you receive. Most of the difference between them lives in details that nobody wants to discuss.

**The emotional beat.** "This offer ends today" is built to shorten your thinking. A good offer will survive a week. If it does not, you have saved yourself money.

## Chapter 6: Time: the possession date and what it does to everything else

**The friend.** The date in the brochure is the most important number in it and the least tested. A building is a long chain of dependent steps, and each link can slip. Treat the promised date as the earliest the home could arrive, not the day it will.

**The philosopher.** Time is the one asset nobody can buy back. A late home costs you months of your life as well as money.

**The mathematician.** Psychologists call the habit of underestimating how long a project will take the planning fallacy. It affects buyers, sellers and builders alike, and it persists even when everyone knows that similar projects ran late. The forecaster's remedy is to look at how comparable projects actually performed and to correct the promise by that record:

> Expected remaining time = promised remaining time × (actual time ÷ promised time on the seller's past projects)

Suppose 24 months remain on the promise, and the seller's past projects took on average 1.4 times as long as promised. Expect about 34 months. Plan your life around 34, not 24.

Then price the wait. Using the monthly cost from Chapter 4 (0.775 a month in that example), ten extra months cost 7.75 before any compensation.

Finally, compare what you have paid with what has been built:

> Payment ratio = money paid ÷ price. Progress ratio = certified work complete ÷ total work.

If the payment ratio is higher than the progress ratio, you are lending to the project. If you have paid 40% and the certified work is 25% complete, you have financed 15 points of construction in advance. Ask for a plan in which payments follow certified progress.

**The engineer.** Ask for the stage-wise construction schedule and the certified progress, signed by the independent architect or engineer. Visit at intervals, take dated photographs and compare them. A site in motion shows people, materials and equipment. A site that is quiet for weeks is a question. Ask what sits on the critical path: approvals, financing, materials, the monsoon or winter season.

**The lawyer.** Four points decide what a delay means:

- **What counts as possession.** Keys, the occupancy certificate, or something earlier?
- **The grace period,** and whether it is added to the promised date.
- **Extensions.** In many markets a regulator or the contract can extend the date. Ask for every extension granted to date and the reasons given.
- **Force majeure.** Which events excuse a delay, and what notice must the seller give?

Then check your own rights: the point at which you may cancel, the refund you receive and whether interest is added, and the time limit for giving notice.

**The consultant.** Ask for the promise in a form you can hold: a month and year in the agreement, not "approximately." Ask for the promised and actual dates of the last three completed projects. Ask what would delay this one. A good answer names specific risks and who owns them. A weak answer is "we always deliver."

**The emotional beat.** A late home disrupts a family's plan: a school year, a lease ending, a promise to parents. Write down where you will live if the date is twelve months later, and what that costs. If you cannot answer, the purchase is too tight.
